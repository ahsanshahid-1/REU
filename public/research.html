SURE-AI · Nature of student activities

What you will actually do

SURE-AI — the Summer Undergraduate Research Experience in AI — engages ten students in hypothesis-driven research on efficient, secure, and trustworthy artificial intelligence. The five pathways below share a common intellectual focus: how AI capability trades off against communication, computation, energy, security, privacy, robustness, data quality, and human control. That shared focus is what lets ten students in different projects learn from one another as a single cohort, in the weekly research exchange and across the summer.

Each applicant ranks the pathways below; we match you with a faculty mentor whose active work fits your interests. Every team follows one protocol: state a precise question, reproduce a working baseline, isolate a student-owned contribution, analyze uncertainty and failure, and produce an approved artifact documenting provenance, intended use, and limitations.

Project 1 · Leads: Dr. Elfikky; Dr. Talburt (data quality & provenance)

Communication-efficient federated adaptation at the edge

When do compressed low-rank updates preserve capability while reducing communication and edge-resource demand under variable bandwidth, participation, and client heterogeneity? Students reproduce a baseline with emulated clients, then compare update-skipping, quantization, compression, client-selection, or aggregation strategies against task quality, bytes transmitted, memory, calibration, and robustness.

Project 2 · Leads: Dr. Milanova (models); Dr. Zhang (deployment); Dr. Basu (visualization)

Efficient multimodal AI from local GPU to edge and optional FPGA

Which combinations of compression, parameter-efficient adaptation, numerical precision, input resolution, and hardware mapping preserve vision or multimodal capability under latency, memory, and energy constraints? Measures include task quality, calibration, throughput, tail latency, peak memory, energy per inference, and cross-platform reproducibility, with a full CPU/simulation fallback.

Project 3 · Leads: Dr. Elfikky (anomaly & drift); Dr. Talburt; Drs. Spann & Basu (analyst-facing evidence)

Data-quality-aware adaptive and explainable cyber defense

How can anomaly detectors adapt to changing normal and malicious behavior without excessive false alarms, forgotten attacks, or opaque high-risk alerts? Using approved public, synthetic, sanitized, or emulated telemetry, teams compare adaptive thresholds, retraining schedules, continual learning, calibrated ranking, and auditable AI-assisted evidence summaries.

Project 4 · Leads: Dr. Basu; Drs. Spann, Talburt & Milanova advising

Trustworthy immersive, wearable, and visual-analytics AI

How can uncertainty, failure, privacy risk, and multi-objective tradeoffs be made visible and actionable? Using approved non-human-subject materials, teams study uncertainty visualization, explanation stability, data minimization, and accessibility. EAC CAVE, VR/MR, and Project Aria resources are used only when they materially enable the question; desktop visualization is a complete fallback.

Project 5 · Leads: Dr. Elfikky (communications models); Dr. Zhang (hardware-aware deployment)

Robust learned space-optical communications with hardware-aware deployment

When do neural channel-estimation, symbol-detection, or autoencoder methods remain calibrated and reliable under turbulence, Poisson noise, and channel shift? Arkansas Space Grant models and code provide nonproprietary baselines; students run a bounded ablation, calibration study, or hardware-aware experiment, with simulation and institutional computing as a complete baseline pathway.

Cross-project synthesis

The five pathways share questions about resource limits, distribution shift, attack exposure, data quality, uncertainty, and appropriate human reliance. Weekly exchanges compare how similar design decisions play out across the projects. Each student owns a defined intellectual component and explains how it changes the team's conclusion — originality through focused intervention, not an unrealistically large system build.

Week by week

  • Weeks 1–2. Orientation covering program expectations and the code of conduct, training in the responsible and ethical conduct of research, and a skills bootcamp (Linux, version control, reproducibility, data stewardship), plus literature analysis, question refinement, and baseline replication, so every student starts from a common baseline.
  • Weeks 3–6. Sustained research with your mentor and team, taking increasing ownership of a bounded intervention, ablation, robustness study, deployment comparison, or failure analysis; a weekly cohort research exchange; and professional development workshops on graduate school, research careers, scientific communication, and responsible AI.
  • Weeks 7–8. Final validation, limitations, documentation, and scientific writing; poster and oral communication; the final research symposium where every student presents; and continuation planning that connects the work to your academic year.

After the summer

  • Mentors continue interacting with students during the academic year, to the extent practicable, to connect the research experience to your overall course of study, as NSF directs.
  • Conference travel support to present your results at a regional or national venue.
  • Recommendation letters and graduate school application guidance from your mentor.
  • Support toward publication when summer results warrant it.
The research environment

Where the work happens

UA Little Rock is a metropolitan public research university that combines doctoral computing programs with specialized research facilities and an access-oriented public mission. SURE-AI uses existing university and independently supported resources; no major equipment is requested from NSF. Before any project is advertised, a Project Readiness Record is approved and environments are tested four to six weeks before students arrive.

  • Faculty mentors. Mentors are drawn from the Department of Computer Science, the Department of Electrical & Computer Engineering, and the university's research centers, selected for expertise and a documented history of involving undergraduates in research, including publishing with undergraduate coauthors.
  • Mentor training. All mentors complete structured training before the summer, covering inclusive research supervision, feedback, authorship, accessibility, and professional conduct, as NSF requires.
  • Monitoring. Mentoring quality is supervised through program-level check-ins during the summer, so it is monitored, not assumed.
  • Sustained mentoring. Mentoring relationships continue after students leave the Site, through academic-year check-ins, publication support, and recommendation letters.

Recruitment, selection & matching

A national pool, with relationship-based outreach

SURE-AI uses NSF ETAP to maintain one national applicant pool. The primary applicant population is students in associate- or baccalaureate-degree programs in computer science, information science, computer engineering, cybersecurity, data science, or closely related CISE fields. Eligibility review is separated from merit review, and each eligible application receives at least two conflict-screened reviews using a calibrated rubric. Publications and advanced equipment access are not prerequisites.

Project management

Team leadership and complete fallback pathways

SURE-AI operates as one coordinated Site of ten participants in five two-student teams, each with a primary faculty lead and a graduate near-peer mentor. The eight-week program runs with weekly leadership meetings and formal scope gates at Weeks 2, 5, and 7. Every project has a confirmed baseline and a complete fallback, so no team depends on specialized hardware, commercial-facility access, or an unconfirmed external role.

Evaluation & reporting

How the program measures success

Professional development and conduct

Development, and a safe, respectful, inclusive environment

NSF requires every REU Site to plan for four things in this area. Here is how this Site meets each one.

  • (1) Student professional development, including training in the responsible and ethical conduct of research, plus workshops on scientific writing, presentation, graduate school, and careers.
  • (2) Mentor selection: research mentors are chosen for expertise and a track record of mentoring undergraduates, documented in the mentor roster above.
  • (3) Mentor training and monitoring: structured training before the summer and program-level check-ins during it, so mentoring quality is supervised, not assumed.
  • (4) Communicating expectations of behavior to ensure a safe, respectful, inclusive, harassment-free environment for all participants.

Code of conduct and harassment policy

NSF does not tolerate sexual harassment, or any other form of harassment, where NSF funded activities take place, and neither do we.

  • All participants in the Site (REU students, faculty, postdocs, graduate students, and other research mentors) attend an orientation covering expectations of behavior.
  • The orientation reviews the university's policy addressing sexual harassment, other forms of harassment, and sexual assault, including reporting and complaint procedures.
  • For any off campus or off site research, the university certifies a plan for a safe and inclusive working environment, per PAPPG Chapter II.E.9.
